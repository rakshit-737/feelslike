1The problem, in plain words

Most office buildings cool every room on a fixed timetable, set to a cold number such as 22 °C, "just in case". Nobody asks the people inside whether they are comfortable. Two things go wrong at once:

  • Energy is wasted. Air-conditioning (HVAC) is the largest single electricity use in an Indian office — roughly 40–60% of the building's electricity, per the LBNL/BEE study cited in our feasibility pack. Over-cooling a room by a degree or two, all day, every day, is expensive.
  • People are still uncomfortable. The same room is too cold for someone by the window and too warm for someone near the projector. The only feedback channel is a facilities ticket that takes hours or days — or a sweater, or a space heater under the desk, which wastes even more energy.

The obvious fix — "just set the thermostat warmer" — saves energy but makes people too hot. The other obvious fix — "add occupancy sensors and a timer" — helps with empty rooms but still cannot hear that this meeting in this room is stuffy right now.

What FeelsLike does differently It treats the occupants as sensors. Anyone can say, in their own words, what feels wrong. The system turns that into a small, time-limited, safety-bounded adjustment for exactly that room, and otherwise runs the building as lean as it safely can. The goal is efficiency without sacrifice: lower energy and fewer uncomfortable minutes.

Why a hackathon cares

The problem statement asks for three things combined: a digital twin of a building, optimisation of how it runs, and natural-language feedback from people. FeelsLike is built around exactly that combination, and after round 1 the jury asked teams for more hardware, more original thinking, and a clearer view of real-world feasibility — which is why this project now includes a real physical sensor node, a cost and payback analysis, and a written list of its own limitations.

2The words you need first

Only a handful of ideas are needed to follow everything else. A longer glossary is at the end.

TermWhat it means here
HVACHeating, Ventilation and Air-Conditioning — the equipment that cools rooms and moves fresh air. In this project it mostly cools (it is modelled on a hot Indian climate).
ZoneOne area the HVAC controls separately, such as a conference room. Our model building has five zones.
SetpointThe target temperature you ask the HVAC to hold, e.g. 25.0 °C. A higher cooling setpoint means less cooling and less energy.
Vent / fan levelHow hard the fan pushes air: 0 (off), 1, or 2. More fan brings more outdoor air (helps with stuffiness) but costs electricity.
kWhKilowatt-hour, the unit on an electricity bill. 1 kWh ≈ running a 1 kW appliance for one hour.
Comfort bandThe temperature range counted as comfortable while people are present: 23.0 °C to 26.5 °C.
Violation-minuteOne minute during which an occupied zone is outside the comfort band. This is how discomfort is measured. An empty room can never violate.
Digital twinA computer model that behaves like the real building: given weather, people and HVAC commands, it computes how each room's temperature changes and how much electricity is used.
NLPNatural Language Processing — getting a computer to understand free text. Here: turning "the cabin is freezing" into zone = Cabin C, issue = too cold, severity = 2.
LLMLarge Language Model — an AI text model (we use one hosted by Groq). It is one component, with an offline fallback so the system never depends on it.
ConstraintThe structured, time-limited instruction created from one complaint: "make Conference Room B about 0.4 °C cooler with more airflow, fading over two hours".
ControllerThe decision logic that, every simulated minute, chooses each zone's setpoint and fan level.

3The idea in one picture

Figure 1. The whole system. Text goes in at the top left; the building responds; the dashboard shows what happened and why.

Two design choices matter more than any other:

  1. The controller is the only thing that writes to the building, and everything it writes passes a hard safety check. Complaints, the AI, humidity and crowd adjustments can only request small offsets; they can never command a dangerous temperature.
  2. Every result comes from a fair A/B race. Two identical twins run side by side on bit-identical weather and identical occupants. One uses FeelsLike, the other uses the typical fixed schedule. The only difference is the controller, so the difference in energy and comfort is caused by the controller.

4Follow one complaint, start to finish

At 14:05 on a Tuesday, someone types into the dashboard: "it's really stuffy in conference room b". Here is everything that happens, in order.

#StepWhat happens
1Privacy scrubPhone numbers and e-mail addresses are removed before the text leaves the server, while zone names and temperatures are protected so the meaning survives.
2ParseThe LLM reads the text and returns strict JSON: zone zone_b, issue stuffy, severity 2, confidence ~0.9. If the AI is unreachable or returns anything invalid, the offline rules parser answers instead, in about a millisecond. Measured live: ~1.9 s via the LLM.
3GuardrailsAn unknown zone is erased (the system never acts on an invented room). No zone named → it asks "which zone?" instead of guessing. Not a comfort complaint ("the projector is broken") → ignored. "No longer stuffy" → the zone's complaints are cleared.
4ConstraintA constraint is filed: stuffy at severity 2 means −0.4 °C and fan +1 for Conference Room B, starting at full weight and fading by half every 45 minutes, gone after 2 hours.
5ArbitrationIf someone else just said room B is freezing, the two are combined by a weighted average (severity × confidence × recency) and marked CONFLICT with a human-readable explanation.
6ControlOn the next simulated minute the controller takes the normal schedule (25.0 °C for an occupied room), adds the −0.4 °C offset (capped at ±1.8), clamps to 21.5–29.0 °C, and raises the fan.
7PhysicsThe twin computes the room's new temperature and the extra electricity the fan and cooling use.
8HardwareConference Room B is also the real zone. Its new fan level is sent to the ESP32 in the reply to its next reading, within ~2 seconds. VERIFIED on the bench: the fan channel lit up and the board echoed fan=1 two seconds later.
9RecordA decision record is logged: what changed, why (reason_code), estimated energy and comfort effect. The feed shows the parsed chip, latency, and whether AI was used.
10LearnIf room B is reported stuffy around the same hour on two different days, comfort memory will start pre-applying a gentle fix 30 minutes early.
The point of this walkthrough A casual sentence becomes a bounded, reversible, explainable action in about two seconds — and at no step can a misunderstanding produce an unsafe command. The worst case of a bad parse is a clarifying question or a small offset that fades on its own.

5The digital twin (the physics)

The building

The twin models a 520 m² office floor with five zones. Each has its own size, orientation (which affects sunshine), heat-storage capacity, insulation, cooling capacity and occupancy pattern.

ZoneNameFacesArea m²Thermal mass C (MJ/K)Envelope UA (W/K)Cooling capacity (W)People pattern
AOpen Office ANorth2203.250016,000office (up to 24)
BConference Room BSouth600.91607,000meetings 10–11, 14–15:30, 16:30–17:30
CCabin CEast400.61104,0003 people, 9–19
DLobby DWest901.43008,000lobby traffic
ECafeteria ESouth1101.634010,000lunch surge

Room B is highlighted because it is the zone mirrored by the real hardware (Section 15).

The model: a room as a leaky bucket of heat

Each zone is a lumped RC thermal model — the same idea as an electrical circuit with a resistor (R) and a capacitor (C). Think of the room's air and furniture as a bucket that holds heat (the capacitance C). Heat leaks in through walls and windows from the hot outdoors (the conductance UA = 1/R). Sun, people and neighbouring rooms add heat. The air-conditioner removes heat. The temperature is how full the bucket is.

Figure 2. What flows into and out of one zone every simulated minute.

Every simulated minute (60 s), for each zone, the twin computes:

T_free = T + (60 s / C) × [ UA·(T_out − T)                   heat leaking in through the envelope
                           + 45·fan·(T_out − T)             outdoor air brought in by ventilation
                           + solar_peak × solar_factor      sunshine (depends on facade and hour)
                           + 100 W × people                  body heat
                           + Σ G_ij·(T_neighbour − T) ]      heat through shared walls

q_cool = min( C·(T_free − setpoint) / 60 s , cooling capacity )   "perfect thermostat, finite capacity"
T_new  = T_free − 60 s · q_cool / C
Power  = q_cool / 3.4 (COP) + fan power {0: 0 W, 1: 150 W, 2: 420 W}
  • Perfect thermostat, finite capacity: the AC removes exactly the heat needed to hit the setpoint — unless that exceeds the unit's capacity, in which case the room drifts above setpoint. That is how real overload shows up.
  • COP 3.4 means one unit of electricity moves 3.4 units of heat out of the room — typical for an efficient split AC.
  • Neighbouring zones share walls with conductances between 40 and 110 W/K, so a hot lobby warms the open office. All zones update simultaneously from the previous minute's values, so the order in which zones are computed never changes the result.
  • Weather is synthetic, hot and deterministic: the same "seed" number always produces exactly the same weather. An optional live-weather fetch exists but never feeds the headline results.
  • Humidity is tracked with a proper moisture balance but is not charged to energy — see Section 18.
Why this simple model and not a full CFD simulation? A lumped RC model is the standard, literature-backed choice for control work: it runs thousands of times faster than real time on a laptop (the dashboard races a week in minutes), it has a handful of parameters that can be measured from a real room (Section 16), and it captures the effects that matter for a setpoint decision — thermal inertia, envelope loss, sun, people and capacity. It does not model air currents, radiant heat or stratification; those gaps are listed as limitations, not hidden.

6Understanding the complaint (NLP)

What the parser must produce

Every message, whatever the language or wording, becomes the same small structure:

{ "is_comfort_complaint": true,
  "zone_id": "zone_b",            the single primary zone (or null)
  "zone_ids": ["zone_b"],         every zone named, for multi-zone messages
  "issue": "stuffy",              too_hot | too_cold | stuffy | humid | drafty | other
  "severity": 2,                  1 mild · 2 clear discomfort · 3 urgent
  "confidence": 0.9,              0 to 1
  "requires_clarification": false,
  "language": "en" }              en | hinglish | mixed

Two parsers, one contract

Primary: the LLM

A system prompt carries the exact schema, the list of real zones with their nicknames, anti-hallucination rules and worked examples (including Hinglish and a "projector is broken" negative). Any OpenAI-compatible provider works; the project currently uses gpt-oss-120b on Groq's free tier, measured at ~1.9 s per message. A 15-second timeout protects the live demo.

Fallback: the offline rules parser

A staged pipeline with no network and no AI: normalise text → detect language → detect retraction → find zones (including typo tolerance) → detect intent → estimate severity → check context (appliances, outdoors) → compute confidence. It answers in about a millisecond, so the demo works on stage even with no internet.

Any failure of the LLM — no key, network error, malformed JSON, a schema violation — silently hands the message to the rules parser, and the feed shows which one answered.

What the parser handles

Kind of messageExampleResult
Plain complaint"It's way too hot in Conference Room B"too_hot, room B
Typos"teh confrence rom is stuffyy"stuffy, room B
Hinglish / Tamil-English"reception aur canteen dono me garmi hai"too_hot, lobby + cafeteria

How good is it — honestly

The benchmark has 70 labelled cases (30 development, 40 held-out). Because the team improved the parser by studying those cases, their scores read close to 100% and are not quoted as real-world accuracy. The number that is quoted comes from a separate blind probe of 20 sentences that were never used for development (Section 14 explains how it is kept honest):

Blind probe v2 (20 unseen cases, measured once)Rules parserLLM (auto mode)
Zone set exactly right90%90%
Complaint vs not-a-complaint60%70%
Issue right65%75%
Everything right at once ("exact triple")45%55%
Why 45–55% is still safe The number that protects the building is zone extraction at 90% — and both zone misses added an extra real zone; neither invented a room. When the zone is unclear the system asks instead of acting. A wrong issue produces at most a ±1.8 °C offset that fades within two hours. What still fails is predictable: fresh metaphors ("greenhouse vibes"), inverted sarcasm ("sponsored by the penguin exhibit") and implied heat in Hinglish. Those failures are committed in the repository, not deleted.

7Constraints: how a complaint becomes an instruction

The effect table

Each issue and severity maps to a fixed setpoint offset and a fan change. Negative offset = cooler.

IssueSeverity 1Severity 2Severity 3Fan
too_hot−0.8 °C−1.3 °C−1.8 °C0
too_cold+0.8 °C+1.3 °C+1.8 °C0
stuffy−0.3 °C−0.4 °C−0.5 °C+1
humid−0.4 °C−0.5 °C−0.6 °C+1
drafty+0.3 °C+0.4 °C+0.5 °C−1

Complaints fade: recency-weighted democracy, not a ticket queue

A complaint is strongest the moment it is made and loses influence over time:

weight = severity × confidence × 0.5^(age / 45 minutes)          and exactly 0 after 2 hours
Figure 3. A complaint's influence over time. Re-reporting creates a fresh, full-strength constraint.

Why this design: if a room stays uncomfortable, people say so again and the new complaint counts in full. If the issue is fixed or the meeting ends, the adjustment quietly disappears and the building returns to its efficient schedule. Nobody's complaint wins forever, and nobody has to close a ticket.

When people disagree

If one person says room B is too hot and another says it's freezing, both constraints stay active and the zone's offset becomes their weighted average (by severity × confidence × recency). The dashboard marks the zone CONFLICT and shows a sentence such as "CONFLICT in zone: too hot (sev 2, 3m ago) vs too cold (sev 3, 1m ago) → severity-weighted compromise +0.4 °C". Because both decay, the compromise shifts naturally as the situation changes.

Human approval and all-clears

  • In the human_approval safety mode a complaint is recorded and visible but moves nothing until an operator approves it. Rejected constraints stay in history but never influence control.
  • An all-clear ("fine now in room B") expires that zone's active constraints immediately, using a separate cleared timestamp so the original complaint time is kept — comfort memory relies on it.

8The controller and its safety envelope

The normal schedule

Without any complaints, the shipped balanced controller runs each zone on an occupancy-aware schedule:

SituationSetpointWhy
People present25.0 °CInside the comfort band, warmer than the typical 22 °C, so far less energy
People expected within 30 minutes26.0 °CPre-cool gently before arrival, with a ventilation flush
Room empty, nobody expected28.5 °CSetback — don't cool air nobody is breathing

The one formula that writes to the building

written setpoint = clamp( schedule(objective)  +  clamp(offset, −1.8 K … +1.8 K) ,  21.5 °C … 29.0 °C )
written fan      = clamp( schedule fan + complaint fan change + crowd boost ,  0 … 2 )

The offset is the only channel through which complaints, humidity or crowding can influence control, and it is clamped twice. Tests pile forty maximum-severity complaints onto one zone under every objective and a broken building, and the written command never leaves the envelope.

Extra adaptive terms

  • Humidity term (max 0.6 K cooler): fires only when a zone has an active humid or stuffy complaint and high humidity — a DX coil only dehumidifies while it is cooling.
  • Crowd term (max 1.0 K cooler): for zones above their design headcount, like the cafeteria at lunch.
  • Crowd fan boost: needs both ≥20 people and ≥80% of design density.

Objectives (operator's choice)

ObjectiveOccupied / pre-cool / empty
comfort24.5 / 25.5 / 28.0
balanced (shipped)25.0 / 26.0 / 28.5
cost, carbon25.7 / 26.7 / 29.0
energy25.9 / 26.9 / 29.0

Cost and carbon share one schedule on purpose: with a flat tariff and a flat grid-carbon factor, minimising rupees and minimising CO₂ are the same problem.

Five safety modes

ModeWhat the controller does
automaticApplies decisions immediately (default).
recommend_onlyComputes and logs decisions but writes only the plain schedule.
human_approvalQueues complaint-driven changes until an operator approves them.
emergency_overrideIgnores occupant constraints and drives every zone to the safe band.
maintenance_lockoutFreezes locked zones at their base schedule. A capacity alert locks its zone automatically, so the controller stops chasing a setpoint a saturated unit can't reach.

9The experiment and the headline result

The setup

Seven simulated days (a Monday-to-Sunday week), weather seed 7, identical occupants, every controller started from the same state. Reproduce with python -m scripts.demo_day.

How to read it

  • The static schedule wastes energy and still fails people: 16,328 violation-minutes, almost all of them over-cooling — rooms colder than 23 °C while occupied.
  • A warmer dumb thermostat saves the most energy but breaks comfort: 31.7% saved, with 429 minutes of people too hot. This is the tempting wrong answer.
  • FeelsLike saves 26.6% with zero violations. It gives up about 5 points of savings versus the reactive thermostat to deliver comfort — that trade is the entire thesis: efficiency without sacrifice.
Say "in simulation" Zero violations holds because the simulated cooling capacity is adequate and the thermostat model is idealised. Both sides of the race share that idealisation, so the comparison is fair — but a real building would not hit exactly zero. The feasibility maths deliberately assumes only 25% of these savings survive in reality (Section 17).

These four numbers are frozen: an automated check re-runs the week after every change, and the project refuses any change that moves them.

10Comfort memory

Some discomfort is predictable. If Conference Room B gets stuffy every afternoon, why wait for someone to complain?

  1. The memory scans real occupant complaints (never its own actions) for the same zone + issue reported on at least 2 different days at a similar time (within ±1 hour).
  2. Thirty minutes before that usual time, it pre-applies a gentle constraint — severity 1, confidence 0.5 — so the fix is small.
  3. It announces the action in the feed: "zone_b usually reports stuffy around 14:00 — pre-applying a gentle fix."
  4. If people already complained that day, it doesn't double up.

This turns a reactive system into a slightly anticipatory one, while staying transparent and bounded by the same envelope.

11Explain, What-if, Maintenance, Analytics

Explainability: "Why did the system change this?"

Every material decision is logged as a structured record — never free-form AI text. A record holds the zone, the old and new setpoint and fan, what the plain schedule would have done, the occupancy, indoor and outdoor temperature, humidity, every constraint that contributed with its current weight, whether there was a conflict, the objective and safety mode in force, an estimated energy change and comfort change, a one-line human summary, and a reason_code from a fixed vocabulary (e.g. precool, setback, constraint_applied, conflict_compromise, clamped, at_capacity). The log keeps the last 500 and only records decisions that actually differ from the previous one for that zone.

What-if: counterfactuals that can't break the live building

An operator can ask questions like "what if occupancy rises 20%?". The engine takes a clone of the live twin and complaint store, applies the change, runs both the unchanged clone and the changed clone forward over the same horizon and weather seeds, and reports the difference with mean, spread and a 95% confidence interval across seeds. Results are labelled measured because the physics really ran. A fingerprint of the live state is checked before and after to prove nothing leaked. Eleven scenarios are built in:

Setpoint +1 °CSetpoint −1 °COccupancy +20%Occupancy −20%
Heat wave +3 °CMonsoon +15 %RHCooling capacity 70%Ignore all complaints
Complaints already expiredObjective: save energyObjective: maximise comfort

Maintenance: faults from evidence, not guesses

Four detectors watch the building: capacity (a unit pinned at 100% while its room stays hot), sensor (stuck, missing or impossible readings), actuator (a commanded change that produces no effect) and recurring (the same complaint again and again). None fires on a single bad reading — each needs sustained evidence (30–45 simulated minutes, with a short grace window) and quotes that evidence verbatim with numbers. Alerts are advisory: they never change control, except that a capacity alert locks its zone so the controller stops demanding cooling the unit cannot give.

Analytics

A bounded rolling sampler (every 15 simulated minutes) feeds a zone-by-hour comfort heatmap (occupied samples only, so an empty room at 3 a.m. is grey, not red), hourly and per-zone energy, complaint statistics (latency, confidence, clarification rate, by issue and zone) and controller activity (interventions, conflicts, clamps).

The Monitor tab is the building-operator view: a zone selector, time windows from live to 7 days, 13 KPI cards, 9 charts with crosshair tooltips, click-to-drill-down, and an alerts table. Its defining rule is that every value carries a badge saying where it came from:

  • CO₂ is estimated, and says so: it uses the ASHRAE 62.1 single-zone mass balance with the twin's own ventilation and leakage rates. It is not a sensor reading.
  • Comfort score (0–100) is a documented heuristic and is explicitly not the ASHRAE PMV comfort index.
  • Thresholds for Normal / Warning / Critical come from one server-side table citing ASHRAE 55 and 62.1, WHO 2021 and EN 12464-1.
  • Noise shows "not instrumented" because nothing in the system measures it — no number is invented.
  • Simulated time and real wall-clock time are never drawn on the same axis.

Time-of-day electricity pricing (display only)

The verified Tamil Nadu commercial tariff (TANGEDCO LT-V, TNERC Order 6/2025) is ₹10.45/kWh at the margin, +25% in peak hours (06–10 and 18–22) and −5% at night (22–05). The dashboard re-prices the same measured energy at that tariff. The controller does not yet shift cooling to cheap hours — doing so would change energy use and invalidate the frozen results — so this view is labelled display-only.

13Reinforcement learning — and why it isn't shipped

Reinforcement learning (RL) trains an agent by trial and error to maximise a reward. Here the reward penalises energy, discomfort and unmet complaints. The team trained a PPO agent (a standard RL algorithm) for 2 million steps; its average reward improved from −91.8 to −86.8 and was still climbing when training stopped.

ComparisonPPO agentShipped rules controller
10 random single days — kWh per day81.684.4
10 random single days — violation-min per day20
7-day week — energy saved29.0%26.6%
7-day week — violation-minutes220
The decision (milestone M4) PPO saves slightly more energy but breaks the zero-violation result. Since "efficiency without sacrifice" is the whole point, the demo ships the explainable rules-based controller, and RL is shown honestly in the Experiments tab as a learning trajectory next to the measured comparison. A longer training run could change this decision; it would be re-measured, not assumed.

14The honesty machinery

A claim is only as good as the process behind it. The project is built so that numbers cannot quietly drift or be inflated.

1 · The rotating blind probe

A test set stops being a fair test the moment you study its failures and fix them. The team caught this happening: after a parser rewrite the held-out split jumped from 55% to 100%, while a fresh set of 20 unseen sentences (probe v1) scored only 50%. Later, when probe v1's own failures were studied to guide fixes, probe v1 was declared "burned": archived with the story written into it, and its later 80% re-score disowned. A new probe v2 was written after all development, with the answers committed before measuring, and run exactly once per parser. The scoring script now refuses to run on a probe marked burned. That is why the quoted numbers are 45% / 55% — lower, but real.

2 · Frozen headline numbers

722.4 / 493.4 / 530.3 kWh and 16,328 / 429 / 0 violation-minutes are re-computed by tests and by a one-command pre-flight check. Any change that moves them is treated as a bug until proven otherwise.

3 · The capability gate

The presentation deck and reports are regenerated by scripts from the result files, so a number can't be typed into a slide and drift from the code. Claims about features (multi-zone, explainability, what-if, hardware, calibration, and others — 11 flags) are only written into the documents after the team has personally verified that feature. As of this document every flag is still off.

4 · Tests

534 automated tests pass. They cover data contracts between modules, the parser, constraint maths, thermal physics, controller safety under abuse, simulator determinism (same seed → bit-identical results), what-if isolation, the HTTP API including concurrency, the hardware bridge and its safety envelope, calibration, pricing, telemetry, and the ambient sensor node.

5 · Labels everywhere

Source badges on every dashboard value; measured vs predicted on every what-if result; a baked-in "SYNTHETIC SELF-TEST — NOT HARDWARE DATA" banner on calibration charts made from synthetic data; "uncalibrated" on sensor readings that haven't been cross-checked; and a written limitations register.

6 · AI used as an enabler, disclosed

The LLM is one measured component with an offline fallback. The feed marks every message that used external AI, and the privacy panel describes exactly what is sent. Physics, control, safety limits, the evaluation policy and the hardware design are engineering decisions the team owns and can defend.

How it talks to the server

Figure 5. The rig's wire protocol.
  • Same controller, same seam. The rig is reached through the same adapter interfaces a real BACnet or Modbus building would use, and its adapters pass the exact conformance test the simulated ones pass. There is no special-case code path.
  • The twin is never overwritten by the real sensor. Doing so would make the A/B race unfair and quietly change the headline numbers. Real readings are displayed, health-checked and logged for calibration instead.
  • Honest capabilities. The rig has a fan but no cooling unit, so a setpoint command to it is rejected and logged ("rig is vent-only"), never faked.

  • Sensing: the node posted every ~2 s with every request accepted, reading about 30.5–31.2 °C and 77–80% humidity, sensor health clean.
  • Control loop: "it is really stuffy in conference room b" → parsed by the LLM → the controller raised zone B's fan → the ESP32 echoed fan=1 within 2 seconds → the blue LED lit.
  • Layer 2: the heater was requested on; once it reached 50% duty the server forced it off (duty_limited: true) and the red LED went dark.
  • Layer 1: the server was killed; the blue LED switched off on its own within the 10-second watchdog; when the server came back the node reconnected and the LED relit. Done twice and seen by eye.

15.5 · The wired ambient node (built, not yet run)

An Arduino Uno with an LM35 temperature sensor will measure the room air around the rig and send it over a USB cable, not Wi-Fi — the same shape as the wired RS-485/Modbus sensor buses in real buildings. A small bridge script forwards its readings. It posts to a separate, sensor-only endpoint whose reply can never contain a command and which can never overwrite the rig's reading (a test proves this). The Uno's internal voltage reference varies by up to ±10% between chips (about ±3 °C), so its readings are labelled uncalibrated until cross-checked against the DHT22 with a provided script. PAUSED until after the intermediate review.

Rehearsal without hardware python -m scripts.mock_node runs a software ESP32 that behaves exactly like the firmware (including the watchdog) around a simulated box. The whole loop can be demonstrated even if the physical rig fails on stage — and it is presented as exactly that.

16Calibration: making the twin match reality

A digital twin is only as good as its parameters. The strongest possible evidence is: "we measured a real box and fitted our model to it, and here is the overlay of measured versus modelled temperature."

How it works

  1. A small heater of known power warms the closed box for a set time, then switches off, while the sensor logs temperature every 2 s.
  2. The fitting script (scripts/fit_rc.py) searches for the insulation R and heat capacity C that make the RC model reproduce that temperature curve (least squares), using the heater's recorded on/off timeline.
  3. It produces a measured-vs-model chart for the deck, plus the fit quality.

What is proven so far

  • On synthetic data with known true values the fitter recovers R and C to within about 0.5%.
  • An end-to-end test runs the whole hardware pipeline in software — including the server's 50% heater duty cap chopping the heating into on/off pulses — and still recovers R and C within 10%.
  • scripts/run_calibration.py drives a real run with one command (about 65 minutes).
Status: PAUSED — needs a heater The fit needs a heater of known wattage (for example a 10 Ω 10 W resistor on 5 V, or 47 Ω 10 W on 12 V). Until one is bought, no calibration result is claimed. Any chart made from synthetic data carries a permanent "NOT HARDWARE DATA" banner.

22Questions a judge will ask

How do you stop the AI from hallucinating a dangerous command?
The AI never writes a setpoint. It only produces a small structured label that is validated against a schema; unknown zones are erased; missing zones trigger a question; and every resulting offset is clamped to ±1.8 K and the setpoint to 21.5–29.0 °C. If the AI fails, the offline parser answers.
Isn't 45–55% parse accuracy low?
It's the exact-everything score on unseen, deliberately hard sentences, measured once. Zone accuracy is 90% with no invented rooms, the system asks when unsure, and a wrong issue causes only a small offset that fades within two hours. We quote the hard number on purpose.
What happens when two people disagree?
Both complaints count, weighted by severity, confidence and recency; the zone is flagged CONFLICT with an explanation, and the compromise shifts as the complaints fade.
How real is the digital twin?
It's a standard lumped RC thermal model with envelope loss, sun by facade, people, ventilation, shared walls and finite capacity. Its parameters can be fitted to a real room, and we built and tested the tools to do that with our hardware.
Why not just use occupancy sensors and a timer?
That handles empty rooms, which our schedule also does. It can't hear that an occupied room is uncomfortable right now, and it can't settle a disagreement. FeelsLike does both, on top of occupancy-aware scheduling.
Why RC and not CFD?
Control needs a model that runs much faster than real time and can be calibrated from a few measurements. CFD is far too slow for that; its gaps are in our limitations list.
How does this attach to a real building?
Offices without a BMS: our nodes plus IR blasters that speak the AC remote's protocol. Offices with one: map five readings and two writes per zone to BACnet or Modbus points, and run our adapter conformance test.
What does it cost and when does it pay back?
About ₹1,050 per zone and about ₹40,000 for a 20-zone floor. Assuming only a quarter of our simulated savings survive, at the verified TANGEDCO tariff, payback is about 4.5 months.
What if a sensor drifts or dies, or the network drops?
Sensor health is inferred from its readings and a bad zone falls back to its schedule. If the network drops, the node's watchdog turns its outputs off within 10 seconds, so the room reverts to plain behaviour — never worse than today.
Would you trust it on a real building?
Three independent safety layers — a physical switch, a firmware watchdog and a server duty cap — plus a clamped control envelope and five safety modes including human approval. Two of the three layers are already tested on real hardware.
What did AI build, and what did you build?
AI assisted with code and powers the parser component, and its use is disclosed in the product. The physics model, the control law and its safety envelope, the arbitration rules, the evaluation policy (the blind probe and capability gate) and the hardware design are engineering decisions the team made and can defend.
Why is RL not your controller?
It saved slightly more energy but caused comfort violations. Zero violations is our thesis, so we ship the explainable controller and show RL as a measured trajectory.
